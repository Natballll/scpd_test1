Exoplanet Discovery as a Model for Biosignature and Technosignature Discovery

The first biosignatures should not be considered to be representative of most life in the universe. Ascertaining this will be a massive scientific feat unto itself.

Even over twenty years after the first exoplanets were discovered, most of our exoplanets catalogued are found very close to their stars. The data might suggest that most exoplanets in the galaxy are thus very close to their host stars, making planets like our own Earth and Jupiter a rarity. In fact, this large number of close-orbiting exoplanets is an artifact of observational bias -- they are the planets that are the easiest to detect. Likewise, the first biosignatures, technosignatures, or any other strand of novel cosmic event will likely be those that are the easiest to detect given our current technological instruments.

Detecting a biosignature with certainty may prove to be a completely different feat than characterizing the kind of life that might be there.

The discovery of exoplanets is premised on an accepted idea of what they must generally all share in common: They have mass, volume, and periodically orbit a star. If a similar model is taken for biosignatures, wherein a list of universal properties of life are adopted and detection methods are devised by working out what the consequences of those properties might be, there could be a situation where a biosignature is detected with high certainty, but scientists are not able to make any further conclusions on what that life might be like in ways that the public might be curious about. This may yield a unique communication challenge. In the realm of biosignature/technosigature detection on other planets, scientists may face longstanding difficulties in characterizing a single signature. Success may look instead like the another frontier of population-level studies of biosignatures that survey many biosignatures, enabling scientists to speak about life across the universe in general, rather than saying for certain life on one planet is like this.
